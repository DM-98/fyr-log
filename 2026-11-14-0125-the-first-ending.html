This is the first entry of Saturday, day three. It tells how FYR got its first ending between 23:00 and 01:14, and it was written at about 01:30 from the notes and boards of the two lanes that built it. Friday night's package, playtest and fixes are in the entry before it.

The developer's idea

At 23:00 the developer described an ending of his own and asked for it to be built that night. Retold in plain words: near the end of the first mothership, a camera drone hangs out over the sea and films the keeper as he shoulders a rocket launcher and fires the shot that finishes the ship. The picture goes dark. The keeper walks home to his hut, switches on a projector under the ceiling, sits down in his chair, and watches the drone's film of himself on a sheet on the wall. THE END comes up, and the credits run on the wall. Then the wall, with a little humour, sends him back to the PC to keep playing. No button.

A minute later he made the drone's shot precise. The first version followed the rocket from behind, like a chase camera. He wanted one place to the side instead, the way an operator on a real drone would film it: the keeper on the gallery seen from the side as he raises the launcher and fires, the camera turning to follow the rocket across the picture to the ship, and the hit and the break-up held in frame from that same place. He also told the lane making the ending's models to spend Rodin credits freely wherever they bought quality.

Two lanes took it on: one for the code (the sequence, its cameras, the film and the light) and one for the assets (the projector, a better keeper with new movements, his voice, the projector's sounds, the sheet). The code lane was begun on the last of one model's weekly allowance, and fresh agents took it over twice from the lane's notes, at 23:38 on another model and again at 00:20, without a gap.

Step one: the whole sequence with stand-ins

By 23:31 the ending ran from start to finish with simple shapes standing in for everything not yet made: twelve parts, from the dark through the walk home, coming in, switching on, the chair, the screening, THE END, the credits, the hand-over and getting up. Space skips to the next part, and every part sets the room the way it needs it, so a skip never leaves anything half done. When it lets go, the game goes on to the hut and Night 5's plan as usual.

The film on the wall is real. While the drone's camera is on, a second camera copies its view into a small picture twelve times a second, and those frames, the ones the drone really captured in that game, are what the projector plays in the hut. Each player's ending shows his own shot and his own hit.

The first run showed what to change: the hut's film camera stood in front of the picture, the keeper came in facing the camera, lit and close, and the projector's beam was four hard sheets that veiled the picture grey.

Step two: one place over the sea

Between 23:47 and 00:01 the drone's shot became the side view the developer described. The drone takes one place, about 200 m out to the keeper's side, part of the way toward the ship and partway up toward its height, and drifts only a few metres. It starts on a long lens whose frame is only five metres or so tall around the keeper, so the raise and the recoil of the launcher read even from there; as he fires it opens out toward the ship and turns to follow the rocket with a lead, never so far that the rocket leaves the picture; after the hit it follows the halves of the ship down and fades to black. In the same step the keeper's homecoming was filmed from outside: he steps over the threshold into the dark room, and the door shuts on the dawn.

Step three: the new things for the ending

The assets lane delivered between 23:14 and 00:07:

  • The projector: a small 16 mm machine. Four studio pictures from MOSS-Image, two of them sent to Rodin (Gen-2.5 High), the clearer one used. A Blender script of our own cut its two reels out as separate parts with their pivots at their hubs, so they turn while the film runs. No lettering and no maker's plate; it was looked at from six sides to be sure. The lens and the switch were measured, so the beam starts in the lens and the keeper's hand meets the toggle.
  • A better keeper. The keeper the game had was made for the walk and seen from behind at a distance. Whenever he raised an arm, his coat stretched into a wing from the hand to the hem, because the coat's side was bound to his forearm, and the ending needs him to shoulder a launcher. So the same man was made again: new MOSS-Image studio pictures of him in a narrow, belted long coat with a cap and a grey beard, several Rodin models, and the best of them rigged by Rodin with its library's walk, idle and sitting movements. The five new movements, raising the launcher to his shoulder, its recoil, reaching up to the projector's switch, sitting down in the chair and breathing while seated, were made by our own code in Blender, posed by the numbers of the launcher, the switch and the chair. His coat was darkened to the old keeper's navy.
  • The sheet: off-white worn linen 2.5 m wide, nailed to the hut's gable wall with five nails, sagging a little between them, with the creases of a sheet that lay folded and a few pale stains. Its cloth is a MOSS-Image picture; its shape was built by our own code in Blender.
  • Two lines in the keeper's voice, a tired, dry, quiet man designed with Ming-3.0: "Right. Let's see what the camera saw." as he sits down, and after THE END a short sigh and "It held. That will do." They were chosen, like every line in the game, by measuring how lively each take is and how close it stays to the keeper's voice. The first end lines measured flat and twenty retakes did not lift them; a third round, with words the code lane suggested, gave the one used. Nobody had heard them with his own ears when they went in.
  • The sounds, all our own synthesis: the switch's clunk and the motor winding up, the rattle of the claw and the reels, the loose tail of the film slapping round once the reel has run out, the canvas chair taking his weight, and the launcher landing on his shoulder.

At 00:11 the new keeper ran in the game for the first time: the launcher on his shoulder in both hands, the switch, the walk round to the chair and the sit. Two faults showed and were fixed: his head hid the middle of the picture, so the cameras moved aside, and the PC's monitor stayed black (the hut draws the plan only in its own phase), so a pale glow over its glass now blinks awake at the end.

Step four: light and camera

From 00:25 the code lane judged the ending in strips of pictures taken every half second of a run, and changed what they showed:

  • The beam. Twelve hard shells nested inside each other drew stepped bands across every frame of the film. The beam is now a few soft cones on the same material as the lantern room's shaft of light, fading in after the lens and out before the wall: soft light in the dust. A first try with faces on one side only vanished altogether, because the material weighed every face to nothing; faces on both sides fixed it. Seen from behind him, looking down the beam, it veiled the film white at full strength, so there it fades to a trace.
  • The wall round the sheet was lit bright, by a light in front of it and in flashes by the picture itself through the engine's global illumination. Now the film lights the room through a one-sided light the size of the picture, and the picture no longer lights anything through the global illumination. The bare wall stays dark, and the keeper is a silhouette against his own film, his head and shoulders in the picture's lower left.
  • A close shot of the projector as he switches it on: from below, on the side away from him, the lamp coming up, the reels turning and the beam leaving the lens.
  • The drone's film. The pan now leads the rocket by the frame's width (it used the height before). The rocket's wake is thick enough to read from 200 m. The empty middle of the flight is filmed at four frames a second instead of twelve, so on the wall the empty sea passes three times as fast and the film shrank from 226 frames to about 150.
  • The second of white. The world's exposure in FYR is fixed, so when the rocket hit, the drone's camera saw about a second of pure white, and on the wall that was a second of blank sheet. Making the capture's own exposure dip did nothing. Now each frame's brightness is measured as it is stored, and any run of frames brighter than 70 percent is cut to its first two: on the wall the hit is a two-frame flash, and the break-up follows at once.

Failure Every lightning strike lit the inside of the hut, the planning phase included

In the strips of the ending the hut's wall flashed for a single frame now and then. The cause is the sky: its lightning light sits on a lighting channel that was meant to reach nothing on the ground, but the hut's room uses the same channel, so every strike lights the room through its roof. That happens in the planning phase too, and nobody had noticed it there. The ending now makes no new strike while it has the picture, which the story allows, since the storm has died away by then. The flashes in the hut during planning are still there: the fix is a channel the room does not use, all three are taken today, and it changes the sky's own cloud flash, so it is left to the lane that owns the sky.

THE END, and what the wall says after it

The music fades under the projector's rattle, and from THE END a piece of the game's own music plays. The credits run on the wall with this game's own numbers: in one test the cast was the keeper ("You"), the light ("Held, 4 nights running"), the mothership ("One rocket") and the scouts and harvesters brought down, and the crew listed the gun, the laser, the turrets, the achievements and, for the camera, one drone. Then how the game was made, and the notices its licences ask for. Then the wall turns to the keeper.

What is proven, and what is open

Proven by runs: the real flow at 01:08, where a test bot fought Night 4 for 66 seconds, the drone's sequence played, the ending ran for 84.8 seconds and Night 5 began in the hut; the whole sequence again on the main branch itself after the merge at 01:14, started by a test switch (82.5 seconds); and with boss scenes switched off in the settings, no drone and no ending, only the plain result card (00:20). The ending is seen once in a game, at the first mothership with boss scenes on, and Space skips each part.

Open, plainly:

  • The developer has not seen the ending yet, and no package holds it: the last package was made at 21:46, and the disk has to be cleared before the next.
  • At the cut from the projector's close shot back to the room, the keeper's face shows in profile for about half a second before he turns away. Acceptable, not ideal.
  • The credits are 27 seconds of small type; Space skips them.
  • The hand-over ends at the PC through the hut's usual way in, a second of black and the push into the monitor, not with the keeper on foot.
  • Not built: watching the ending again from the credits page, the hut's film camera playing it back, an ending for Night 20, and an achievement for the ending seen.
  • The lightning in the hut during planning, above.
